Terminology

Universal Design and Inclusive Design

Often used interchangeably in higher education. Both are frameworks for designing content, activities, and assessments that meet the needs of all learners, whatever their academic background, ability, disability, race, ethnicity, class, gender, age, sexual orientation, veteran status, or the way they prefer to learn.

Our focus today is students with disabilities. The UDL framework also addresses the needs of other historically marginalized and disadvantaged students, and we will name those points as they come up.

Terminology

Accessibility

Minimizing barriers and maximizing access so people can participate fully. In other words, repairing the mismatch between a person and their environment.

“Accessible” means a person with a disability is afforded the opportunity to acquire the same information, engage in the same interactions, and enjoy the same services as a person without a disability in an equally effective and equally integrated manner, with substantially equivalent ease of use.

CAST, AEM Center

Terminology

Assistive technology

Anything used to perform a function that would otherwise be difficult or impossible. Rarely visible to you from the front of a room.

Screen readers

Read on-screen text aloud in a synthetic voice. Depend entirely on how your document is structured.

Magnification

Enlarges screen content. Fixed-width layouts and images of text break it.

Alternative input

Large-key keyboards, specialised mice, switch access, dictation.

Mobility devices

Walkers and wheelchairs are assistive technology too, and rooms are designed as if they are not.

University of Washington

The idea that does the work

Systemic barriers

Policies, procedures, or practices that unfairly discriminate and can prevent individuals from participating fully in a situation. Systemic barriers are often put into place unintentionally.

Yakobi, 2013

In a college classroom, that can look like:

  • Learning objectives that are unclear or poorly defined
  • Language and images that do not represent the students in the room
  • Only one permitted way to demonstrate knowledge
  • Information presented one way only, spoken or written, with no alternative
  • Everyone moved through complex new material at one pace, with no support at either end

Where most of us are now

The accommodation model

Equity is real progress over equality. It is also consumable: it must be re-bought every time.

  • Access is treated as the individual's problem, to be solved by them, the instructor, and disability services
  • Access is achieved by special accommodation, or by retrofitting what already exists
  • Access is reactive, and often delivered in a separate place or by different treatment
  • Access must be reconsidered for every new person who arrives

Gill, 1994

The principle

What is essential for some is almost always good for all.

Meyer, Rose & Gordon, 2014, p. 51

Curb cuts

Built for wheelchair users. Used by anyone with a stroller, a delivery cart, a bike, or a suitcase.

CAST UDL Guidelines 3.0

Three principles, three questions

Engagement

Why would a student care?

Multiple ways to become and stay involved: choice, relevance, collaboration, belonging, feedback.

Representation

How does the content reach them?

Multiple ways to perceive and understand: format, language, symbols, building on what they know.

Action & expression

How do they show you?

Multiple ways to act and demonstrate: media, tools, strategy, progress they can monitor.

The goal is learner agency: purposeful and reflective, resourceful and authentic, strategic and action-oriented.

CAST, 2024

CAST UDL Guidelines 3.0

The goal of UDL is learner agency that is purposeful and reflective, resourceful and authentic, strategic and action-oriented. Rows are the three bands: access, support, and executive function.

Design Multiple Means ofEngagement

Access

Welcoming Interests & Identities (7)

  • Optimize choice and autonomy (7.1)
  • Optimize relevance, value, and authenticity (7.2)
  • Nurture joy and play (7.3)
  • Address biases, threats, and distractions (7.4)

Support

Sustaining Effort & Persistence (8)

  • Clarify the meaning and purpose of goals (8.1)
  • Optimize challenge and support (8.2)
  • Foster collaboration, interdependence, and collective learning (8.3)
  • Foster belonging and community (8.4)
  • Offer action-oriented feedback (8.5)

Executive Function

Emotional Capacity (9)

  • Recognize expectations, beliefs, and motivations (9.1)
  • Develop awareness of self and others (9.2)
  • Promote individual and collective reflection (9.3)
  • Cultivate empathy and restorative practices (9.4)

Design Multiple Means ofRepresentation

Access

Perception (1)

  • Support opportunities to customize the display of information (1.1)
  • Support multiple ways to perceive information (1.2)
  • Represent a diversity of perspectives and identities in authentic ways (1.3)

Support

Language & Symbols (2)

  • Clarify vocabulary, symbols, and language structures (2.1)
  • Support decoding of text, mathematical notation, and symbols (2.2)
  • Cultivate understanding and respect across languages and dialects (2.3)
  • Address biases in the use of language and symbols (2.4)
  • Illustrate through multiple media (2.5)

Executive Function

Building Knowledge (3)

  • Connect prior knowledge to new learning (3.1)
  • Highlight and explore patterns, critical features, big ideas, and relationships (3.2)
  • Cultivate multiple ways of knowing and making meaning (3.3)
  • Maximize transfer and generalization (3.4)

Design Multiple Means ofAction & Expression

Access

Interaction (4)

  • Vary and honor the methods for response, navigation, and movement (4.1)
  • Optimize access to accessible materials and assistive and accessible technologies and tools (4.2)

Support

Expression & Communication (5)

  • Use multiple media for communication (5.1)
  • Use multiple tools for construction, composition, and creativity (5.2)
  • Build fluencies with graduated support for practice and performance (5.3)
  • Address biases related to modes of expression and communication (5.4)

Executive Function

Strategy Development (6)

  • Set meaningful goals (6.1)
  • Anticipate and plan for challenges (6.2)
  • Organize information and resources (6.3)
  • Enhance capacity for monitoring progress (6.4)
  • Challenge exclusionary practices (6.5)

Practical ideas · open the one you need

What this looks like tomorrow

Everything here is something a colleague already does. Open a pillar and take one.

Variety in teaching and learning activities

  • Active learning tasks that allow participation, exploration and experimentation
  • Discussions and small group activities inside lecture classes
  • Engagement material embedded in lecture notes, such as sample exam questions
  • Multiple opportunities to learn through practice
  • Prompt, regular feedback, given early enough that students can act on it
  • Accept a rough draft of a paper or assignment

Interaction with others

  • A class climate in which student diversity is respected
  • A pre-course survey in the LMS, so you know who is in the room
  • Co-construct a community agreement at the start, and return to it during the term
  • Varied social learning: in-class and online discussion, problem-based and inquiry-based work
  • Office hours in flexible formats, in person, by phone, by web conference, booked or drop-in

Use of technology

  • The online environment for small group work, discussion, articles, practice questions, profiles
  • Tools that let students find each other and form study groups
  • Online knowledge checks that give immediate feedback rather than grades

Student choice of course content

  • An optional unit or topic after the required ones are covered
  • Groups research and present on different topics
  • A video as an alternative or supplement to a textbook chapter
  • Questions that guide and focus the reading

Self-regulation and motivation

  • Goal setting at the start of the course or assignment
  • Rubrics given at the beginning, to prompt self-assessment
  • Checklists so students can track their own progress
  • Meaningful, authentic assignments drawn from real practice
  • Outcome or competency based assessment
  • Experiential learning built into the course

Accessible course materials

  • Common file formats that work with text-to-speech
  • A copy of the text on reserve in the library
  • Links to Creative Commons resources, and OER where it fits
  • Slides, readings and materials posted in advance where appropriate
  • Electronic equivalents of paper handouts
  • Captions for graphics
  • Closed captions and transcripts for video

Multimodal sources of information

  • Visual aids alongside lecture and reading: photographs, video, diagrams, simulations
  • Required readings augmented with audio and video
  • Recordings of lectures where permitted
  • An audio file explaining a major assignment
  • Models and graphics as well as text
  • Interactive questions embedded in videos

Pedagogical approaches

  • Vary the route into a concept: logic, statistics, narrative, case study, multiple perspectives, testimonial

Student-created materials

  • Graphic organizer summaries made by students
  • Concept maps, metaphors, illustrations, storyboards
  • Students post class notes to the course site
  • Students build their own glossary of terms across the term

Comprehension and key concepts

  • A syllabus that states outcomes, content, assessment and expectations plainly
  • An advance organizer or study guide for the week ahead
  • An agenda and key concepts at the start of each class
  • Practice exercises with solutions
  • Patterns and themes between ideas made explicit
  • A posted list of frequently asked questions

Check for understanding

  • Summarize key points and tie them back to course outcomes
  • Hinge questions or predictions to review the lesson
  • Online discussion forums
  • Question and answer in class
  • Student response systems to check comprehension and steer discussion

Assignments and demonstration of skills

  • A mix of assessment: formative quizzes, case studies, model building, oral presentation
  • Presentations in class or online
  • Role play, debate, discussion as ways of demonstrating skill
  • Opportunities to develop skills in real settings

Exams

  • A variety of question types: multiple choice, matching, short answer, equations, labelling a diagram
  • Questions that assess remembering, applying and evaluating, not recall alone
  • Graphics incorporated into some questions

Opportunities for interaction and feedback

  • Technologies that support class communication and participation
  • Discussion boards, podcasts or blogs for students who need time to reflect
  • Polling so students can take part without being called out
  • Textbook question sets as practice
  • In-class peer feedback, rubrics, student-led study groups
  • Cumulative assignments with feedback at stages

Student choice

  • Options for demonstrating mastery of the same outcomes
  • Choice of format: paper, presentation, website, poster, video, podcast
  • Choice of due date or topic
  • Tools that reduce barriers: assistive technology, spelling and grammar support, dictation

Assessment anxiety

  • Help students build deliberate strategies for learning
  • Goal setting and strategic competency development
  • Support students in monitoring their own progress
  • Assignment guidelines that state expectations
  • Templates or outlines where appropriate
  • A take-home final where appropriate
  • Sample assignments showing feedback and how they were graded

Audit one real thing

Open one artifact from your own course: a syllabus, a slide deck, a video, or an assignment. Work through this against that one thing. Your answers stay on your device. They are saved in this browser so a refresh will not lose them, and nothing is ever sent to anyone. On a shared computer, use Clear saved answers when you finish.

Work down the list. Clear the basic access items first: they are required, and most take minutes. The UDL items underneath are design decisions, and one of them is worth more than all the rest ticked in a hurry.
Each item cites its source, a WCAG success criterion or a CAST Guidelines 3.0 consideration, so you can look it up or point to it.
